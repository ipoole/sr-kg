From an action to a local rule

A field equation tells us which field configurations are dynamically allowed. How can a rule comparing whole field configurations produce a differential equation at each event?

Consider one smooth real scalar field \(\phi(x)\) in a fixed spacetime region \(\Omega\). Hold the field fixed on its boundary. Choose a Lagrangian density depending on the field, its first derivatives and, possibly, the coordinates. Gauge constraints and higher-derivative theories are outside this example.

The local field equation

We adopt the physical condition that the action is stationary for every allowed infinitesimal variation of the field. Applying the procedure above, with the boundary variation zero, gives

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} -\partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\right) \overset{\text{stationary action}}{=}0. \]

This is the field Euler–Lagrange equation. It holds locally throughout the interior, rather than merely imposing one total constraint on the whole region.

Algebra: varying the field

Write a nearby field as \(\phi_\varepsilon=\phi+\varepsilon\eta\), where the smooth variation \(\eta\) vanishes on the boundary. Define \(\delta S\coloneqq(dS[\phi_\varepsilon]/d\varepsilon)_{\varepsilon=0}\).

Since \(\delta(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial_\mu\eta\), the chain rule gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta S&=\int_\Omega\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}\eta+P^\mu\partial_\mu\eta \right]d^4x,\\ P^\mu&\coloneqq\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}. \end{aligned} \]

Integrating the second term by parts moves its derivative onto \(P^\mu\).

Why does the derivative move?

The product rule says

\[ \partial_\mu(P^\mu\eta)=(\partial_\mu P^\mu)\eta+P^\mu\partial_\mu\eta. \]

Rearrange it and integrate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_\Omega P^\mu\partial_\mu\eta\,d^4x &=\int_{\partial\Omega}P^\mu\eta\,d\Sigma_\mu\\ &\quad-\int_\Omega(\partial_\mu P^\mu)\eta\,d^4x. \end{aligned} \]

The directed surface element \(d\Sigma_\mu\) converts the divergence integral into a boundary integral. The minus sign comes from rearranging the product rule. The boundary term is zero because \(\eta=0\) there.

Consequently,

\[ \delta S=\int_\Omega\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}-\partial_\mu P^\mu \right]\eta\,d^4x. \]

Stationarity requires this to vanish for every allowed variation. Smooth variations supported inside arbitrarily small interior regions can be chosen freely. For a continuous bracket, the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations therefore makes the bracket zero at every interior point.

This gives the displayed field equation. It does not say that each original contribution to the action is zero.

The same conclusion has a useful physical picture.

A physical picture: many small adjustments

Picture a field configuration as values assigned throughout spacetime. We can make a small smooth adjustment in one interior region while leaving distant values unchanged. If some such adjustment changed the action to first order, the configuration would not be stationary.

Requiring stationarity against every local adjustment forces a local condition. The derivatives in the density couple neighbouring values, which is why the condition is a differential equation.

These are comparisons between trial histories, not a process in which the field searches for the smallest action or communicates instantly across spacetime. The picture explains the locality step; it does not replace the algebra or establish the theory’s causal properties.

For a relativistic theory, the action must also have the appropriate Lorentz covariance. That constrains our choice of density; it is not established by the variational calculation alone.
