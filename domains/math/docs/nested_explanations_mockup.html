Temperature, motion and covectors

A teaching prototype for Cotangent space. Read the short argument first, or open explanations wherever you want more detail. Opening an explanation keeps your place; shared explanations have independent fold states.

The scientists and their terrain

Our scientists study a bumpy two-dimensional ground surface, measuring temperature and motion along the ground. Each scientist anchors a rigid flat board to a small patch, tangent at its reference point. Nearby ground points receive coordinates by a smooth, one-to-one projection onto the board’s grid. The board labels the ground; it does not flatten it.

Their grids can have different orientations and scales, even oblique axes. On overlaps they agree smooth conversions between coordinates. The boards are a practical way to imagine local charts, not a requirement that every surface have one global grid.

Assume smooth, time-independent temperature and a smooth probe route, with time measured in seconds. We are finding temperature change due to motion, not changes of weather at a fixed point.

A direction asks a question of the temperature field

Temperature gives one number \(T(p)\) at each ground point. But a probe’s next reading depends on how it moves from that point. Motion along an isotherm gives zero instantaneous change; another direction can give a positive or negative rate.

At a point \(p\), a tangent vector \(v\) describes the probe’s instantaneous motion. We can describe what this vector does without choosing a grid:

For our temperature field, the differential \(dT_p\) gives precisely that readout:

\[dT_p(v)\coloneqq v[T].\]

At a fixed point this rule is linear in the tangent vector. A linear rule from \(T_pM\) to the scalars is a covector; the space of all those rules is the cotangent space \(T_p^*M\).

Why is the readout linear?

At a fixed point, tangent vectors act as directional-derivative operators. Their linear combinations act on a scalar function by

\[(a v+b w)[T]=a\,v[T]+b\,w[T].\]

Consequently \(dT_p(a v+b w)=a\,dT_p(v)+b\,dT_p(w)\), the required covector linearity. The scalars \(a,b\) here are numerical weights. This is linearity in the tangent vector at \(p\); temperature need not be a linear function throughout the patch.

Why is linearity at one point enough?

A differential retains the first-order part of a smooth function at that point. Higher-order terms can matter over a finite journey without changing the linearity of this local first-order readout. Different points generally have different differentials.

This is one important source of covectors. It does not assert that every covector field over a region is the differential of one scalar field.

The same readout on a board’s grid

To calculate it, choose local coordinates \(x^1,x^2\). The coordinates label the same ground points that the probe visits:

The coordinate basis vectors are \(\partial_1,\partial_2\). Their dual covectors \(dx^1,dx^2\) read the corresponding vector components:

\[\begin{aligned}dx^a(\partial_b)&\coloneqq\delta^a{}_b,\\dx^a(v)&=v^a.\end{aligned}\]

Write the temperature differential and the motion in these bases. At \(p\),

\[\begin{aligned}dT_p&=(\partial_aT)_p\,dx^a,\\v&=v^a\partial_a,\\dT_p(v)&=(\partial_aT)_p\,v^a.\end{aligned}\]

Here \(a\) is summed over 1 and 2. The last expression is the familiar chain-rule temperature rate \(dT(\gamma(t))/dt\), evaluated at the point being crossed.

Algebra: where does the component sum come from?

Apply the covector to the vector, using linearity and the dual-basis rule:

\[\begin{aligned}dT_p(v)&=(\partial_aT)_p\,dx^a(v^b\partial_b)\\&=(\partial_aT)_p\,v^b\delta^a{}_b\\&=(\partial_aT)_p\,v^a.\end{aligned}\]

The Kronecker delta selects matching components. No inner product or metric has entered the calculation.

A measurement the scientists can compare

Suppose the local derivatives are \(\partial_1T=2\,\mathrm{K/m}\) and \(\partial_2T=-1\,\mathrm{K/m}\). The probe has coordinate rates \(v^1=0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(v^2=0.2\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Its temperature rate is

\[\begin{aligned}dT_p(v)&=(2)(0.5)+(-1)(0.2)\\&=0.8\,\mathrm{K/s}.\end{aligned}\]

The probe is warming, even though one contribution is cooling. These are coordinate rates, not a claim that the probe’s physical speed is the Euclidean length of that component pair on an arbitrary board.

Why does a centimetre grid give the same answer?

Relabel both coordinate axes from metres to centimetres. The numerical coordinate labels become 100 times larger, and the probe’s component rates become \(50\) and \(20\,\mathrm{cm/s}\). The temperature derivatives become \(0.02\) and \(-0.01\,\mathrm{K/cm}\). Hence

\[\begin{aligned}(0.02)(50)+(-0.01)(20)\\=0.8\,\mathrm{K/s}.\end{aligned}\]

The numerical changes compensate because both descriptions concern the same motion through the same temperature field. The result has units K/s; coordinate independence does not mean dimensionlessness.

This instantaneous rate is exact under the stated assumptions. Using it to estimate a finite journey’s temperature change would be an approximation unless the rate stayed constant.

For intuition, think of \(dT_p\) as a linear readout attached to this point: give it a tangent vector and it returns that vector’s instantaneous temperature rate. It is a rule acting on arrows, not another arrow requiring a dot product.

No metric was needed to pair the differential with the motion. Asking instead for the direction of greatest temperature increase at a fixed physical speed would require a metric to define that speed.
